## A01 — Game Development Isn't a Game
*A: Work Ethic · 04:06 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jlFkxfkICO8)*

**Technique:** Target-Driven Design Detached from Personal Preference — Designers should treat each game project as an objective assignment defined by its target audience and genre goals rather than their personal tastes or skill levels. By understanding universal mechanics of fun, a creator can effectively design games in genres they do not personally play or excel at.
- ✅ Define target audience player skills and constraints explicitly before establishing mechanics (e.g., beginner-oriented accessibility vs. hardcore mastery).
- ✅ Separate your personal playing proficiency from mechanical design decisions to avoid balancing exclusively around expert biases.
- ✅ Approach every development task—even unappealing themes or unfamiliar genres—by analyzing what intrinsically engages the intended audience.
- ❌ Designing games strictly for your own taste and skill level when targeting a broader or different demographic.
- ❌ Allowing personal distaste for an assigned genre, theme, or visual style (e.g., realistic horror) to compromise execution quality.
**Technique:** Entertain-First Decision Anchor — When commercial pressures, grueling production challenges, or creative differences cause a team to lose direction, all design conflicts should be resolved against a single north star: delivering entertainment to the player. Grounding decisions in end-user enjoyment maintains development focus and te
- ✅ Use player entertainment value as the final tiebreaker when design, technical, or production priorities conflict.
- ✅ Periodically evaluate feature scope and mechanical complexity by asking if it directly amplifies player enjoyment or merely adds bloat.
- ❌ Letting developer ego, commercial metrics, or production fatigue overshadow the fundamental goal of delivering player joy.
> I think the majority would say, "Because I like games." Which is how it should be.
>
> But once money, sales, and numbers get involved, you need to approach it like any other job.
>
> In a perfect world, we should be free to make the games we want to make.
>
> But even if it's not your cup of tea, a job is still a job—you have to do it, and try to do it well.
>
> In other words, I don't design games for myself.
