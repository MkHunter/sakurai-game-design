## G01 — Assigning Animations
*G: Animation · 04:39 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fV8xIP480qk)*

**Technique:** Frame-Definitive Animation Spec Sheets — Before animation production starts, the director writes a comprehensive animation list defining the push-and-pull timing for every character action. Each entry pairs descriptive movement prose with hard frame values for total duration, hitbox emergence (startup), hitbox deactivation, and cancel wind
- ✅ Catalog every character action in an animation list before 3D rigging or animation begins.
- ✅ Specify exact numeric frames for attack activation (hitbox startup) and termination (deactivation) directly in the text specification.
- ✅ Mark the earliest frame where an attack is cancellable back into neutral or other actions.
- ✅ Label cycling movements (idle, walk, multi-hit holds) as 'Loop' or 'Any' (任意) rather than binding them to rigid frame durations.
- ❌ Letting animators produce motions without agreed-upon startup and recovery frame budgets, forcing retroactive re-timing that distorts animation curves.
- ❌ Omitting combat intent and hitbox timings from early motion specs, relying solely on artistic intuition.
**Technique:** Articulated Mannequin Silhouette Specification — To communicate dynamic poses clearly without requiring early 3D block-ins, physically pose an articulated action figure or 3D dummy, photograph it, cut out the silhouette, and boost its contrast on spec sheets. Annotate these key poses with motion arcs, weapon angles, and camera-relative adjustments
- ✅ Generate 70 to 80 pages of visual spec sheets per fighter, pairing high-contrast posed mannequins with functional design requirements.
- ✅ Draw visual attack arcs and weapon trajectories over mannequin poses to indicate intended reach, trajectory, and hitbox shapes.
- ✅ Adjust weapon angles relative to the camera on spec sheets (e.g., tilting long weapons forward or backward) to avoid unreadable silhouettes or screen-space clipping.
- ✅ Clearly annotate character-specific mechanical constraints, such as requiring an underhand throw so a projectile rotates along the ground correctly.
- ❌ Relying purely on vague verbal descriptions like 'a strong slash' or 'a heavy dodge' when commissioning key combat actions.
- ❌ Assuming physical or digital reference mannequins represent the final movement limit, ignoring their physical joint restrictions compared to stylized game rigs.
**Technique:** Source-Adaptive Visual Referencing — Vary reference documentation depending on character morphology and heritage. Humanoid fighters benefit from posed mannequins, retro/crossover fighters require direct source-game sprite/model screenshots to preserve signature mannerisms, and non-humanoid characters should omit visual proxies in favor
- ✅ Include screenshots of original source-material frames (e.g., retro 2D sprites) directly on spec cards when translating legacy movesets into 3D.
- ✅ Omit visual reference figures entirely for non-humanoid characters (like Piranha Plant) and rely purely on descriptive behavioral text rather than forcing ill-fitting dummy poses.
- ✅ Detail speed-dependent animation variants in spec notes (e.g., rigid straight posture at high flight speed vs. flailing legs at low speed).
- ❌ Forcing humanoid pose mannequins onto non-humanoid, amorphous, or plant-like creatures, producing unnatural and distorted initial animator passes.
- ❌ Attempting to re-invent iconic crossover character attacks from memory instead of supplying direct 1:1 sprite reference frames.
**Technique:** All-Hands Cross-Team Animation Feedback — Conduct animation review and instructional sessions with all animators present simultaneously rather than giving fragmented 1-on-1 critiques. Collective feedback ensures that corrections, timing principles, and common pitfalls are absorbed by the entire team, lifting collective craft quality and pre
- ✅ Assemble the entire animation team when giving directorial feedback and practical critique sessions.
- ✅ Use individual animation reviews to explain broader mechanical and aesthetic principles applicable across the full roster.
- ✅ Document and share practical pointers openly so that a solution discovered for one character prevents missteps across others.
- ❌ Silently correcting or critiquing animators in private 1-on-1 silos, forcing the lead to repeat identical feedback dozens of times across different team members.
- ❌ Over-policing every nuance when an idea proves physically or technically impractical; learn when to let an idea go if it cannot achieve the required gameplay feel.

## G03 — Breaking Down Attack Animations
*G: Animation · 03:35 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LewXWM7HDd8)*

**Technique:** First-Frame Snap for Attack Anticipation — When initiating an attack, jump immediately to an exaggerated windup or lead-in pose on frame one rather than smoothing the interpolation from the standby pose. This instantaneous visual pop gives the attacking player immediate input confirmation while simultaneously telegraphing the incoming attack
- ✅ Snap to a broad, distinct silhouette on the very first frame of an attack input without easing or blending frames from standby.
- ✅ Ensure the lead-in pose has high visual contrast against the standby pose to maximize read speed in competitive action.
- ❌ Smoothing or cross-fading the transition between standby and lead-in, which adds visual latency and delays player input acknowledgment.
**Technique:** Held Follow-Through with Actionable Cancel Frames — Follow-through poses represent the vulnerable cooldown after an active strike and should be held in an unmistakable posture that looks distinctly different from standby. By implementing cancel frames toward the end of recovery, players can transition into new actions early without waiting for the fu
- ✅ Design follow-through poses to look visibly vulnerable and hold them static as long as possible while the character is locked.
- ✅ Assign cancel frames before the animation fully completes so players can move, jump, or attack without waiting for the character to return to neutral idle.
- ✅ Ensure the cancel window provides sufficient 'elbow room' to keep gameplay feeling fluid while preserving commit risk.
- ❌ Forcing the player to wait until the full recovery animation finishes and the character returns to neutral standby before regaining control, which causes games to feel rigid.
**Technique:** Four-Parameter Combat Animation Specification — To streamline combat animation production between design and art, specify exactly four concrete parameters: the standby pose, the attack pose, the attack active frame (startup), and total/cancel frames. These four metrics firmly anchor the timing constraints and key silhouettes while leaving animato
- ✅ Establish standardized ground and aerial standby poses first, and forbid altering them once production commences.
- ✅ Define the exact active startup frame and total/cancel frame counts in design specs before animators begin tweening.
- ✅ Provide explicit reference illustrations for the peak attack pose and neutral standby pose to bookend the motion.
- ❌ Giving animators open-ended timing requests without rigid startup and cancel frame counts, leading to mismatched hitboxes and balance drift.
- ❌ Constantly tweaking base idle/standby poses mid-production, which breaks consistency across all branching attacks.
> Almost all of our animations are built from ground or aerial standby poses, so it's vital we nail these down first and don't change them.
>
> In my games, these animations often start with a big, unexpected movement on the very first frame.
>
> Naturally, this is the key pose of an attack.
>
> Additionally, we also assign "cancel frames" to follow-throughs.
>
> Now, as I've touched on before, whenever we make new animations, I always specify the following four things: the standby pose, the attack pose, the attack start frame, and the total frames—or the time until the attack can be canceled.
